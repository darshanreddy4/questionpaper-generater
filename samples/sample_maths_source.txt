Chapter 1: Linear Equations

A linear equation in one variable is an equation of the form ax + b = c, where a, b and c are constants and a is
not zero. Solving a linear equation means finding the value of x that makes the equation true.

Example: Solve 3x + 7 = 22.
Subtract 7 from both sides: 3x = 15.
Divide both sides by 3: x = 5.

Linear equations are used to model simple real-life situations such as sharing costs, calculating ages and finding
unknown quantities in everyday problems.


Chapter 2: Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation is a polynomial equation of degree two, written in the standard form ax^2 + bx + c = 0, where
a is not zero. The roots of a quadratic equation can be found by factorisation, by completing the square, or by the
quadratic formula x = (-b +/- sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)) / 2a.

The discriminant of a quadratic equation is defined as D = b^2 - 4ac. If D is greater than zero the roots are real
and distinct, if D equals zero the roots are real and equal, and if D is less than zero the roots are not real.

Example: Find the roots of x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0.
Factorise: (x - 3)(x - 4) = 0.
So x = 3 or x = 4.


Chapter 3: Arithmetic Progressions

An arithmetic progression is a sequence of numbers in which the difference between any two consecutive terms is
constant. This constant is called the common difference, usually written d. If the first term is a, the nth term of
an arithmetic progression is given by a_n = a + (n - 1)d.

Example: Find the 10th term of the arithmetic progression 3, 7, 11, 15, ...
Here a = 3 and d = 4.
a_10 = 3 + (10 - 1) x 4 = 3 + 36 = 39.


Chapter 4: Triangles

Figure 1: A right-angled triangle ABC, with the right angle at B, side AB vertical, side BC horizontal, and the
hypotenuse AC joining A to C.

The Pythagoras theorem states that in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of
the squares of the other two sides. If AB and BC are the two legs of a right-angled triangle and AC is the
hypotenuse, then AC^2 = AB^2 + BC^2.

Example: In a right-angled triangle, AB = 6 cm and BC = 8 cm. Find AC.
AC^2 = 6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100.
AC = 10 cm.

Two triangles are called similar triangles when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides
are in the same ratio. This is also known as the basic proportionality theorem (Thales theorem): if a line is drawn
parallel to one side of a triangle intersecting the other two sides, it divides those two sides in the same ratio.

Figure 2: Triangle ABC with a line DE drawn parallel to BC, where D lies on AB and E lies on AC, showing the
proportional segments AD, DB, AE and EC.


Chapter 5: Coordinate Geometry

The distance between two points A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2) in the coordinate plane is given by the distance formula:
AB = sqrt((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2).

Example: Find the distance between the points A(2, 3) and B(5, 7).
AB = sqrt((5 - 2)^2 + (7 - 3)^2) = sqrt(9 + 16) = sqrt(25) = 5 units.

A straight line can be written in the form y = mx + c, where m is the slope and c is the y-intercept. The graph of a
straight line crosses the x-axis and the y-axis at points that can be found by substitution.

Figure 3: A straight line graph on the coordinate plane, sloping upward from left to right, crossing the y-axis
above the origin and the x-axis to the right of the origin.


Chapter 6: Trigonometry

Trigonometry is the branch of mathematics that studies the relationships between the angles and sides of triangles.
For a right-angled triangle, tan(angle) is defined as the ratio of the side opposite the angle to the side adjacent
to the angle.

The angle of elevation is the angle formed by the line of sight with the horizontal when an observer looks upward
at an object. The angle of depression is the angle formed when the observer looks downward.

Figure 4: A vertical tower BT standing on the ground, with a point C on the ground some distance from its foot, and
the angle of elevation marked at C between the ground and the line of sight to the top of the tower T.

Example: A tower stands vertically. From a point 20 m from its foot, the angle of elevation of the top is 45
degrees. Find the height of the tower.
tan 45 = height / 20, and since tan 45 = 1, the height of the tower is 20 m.


Chapter 7: Circles and Mensuration

The area of a circle of radius r is given by the formula area = pi r^2, and the circumference of a circle is given
by 2 pi r. Using pi = 22/7 makes the arithmetic exact for radii that are multiples of 7.

Example: Find the area of a circle of radius 14 cm.
Area = 22/7 x 14 x 14 = 616 square cm.

The area of a triangle is given by area = 1/2 x base x height.

A cylinder is a solid with two parallel circular bases joined by a curved surface. The volume of a cylinder of base
radius r and height h is given by volume = pi r^2 h, and its curved surface area is given by 2 pi r h.

Figure 5: A right circular cylinder with its base radius and height both labelled.

Example: Find the volume of a cylinder with base radius 7 cm and height 10 cm.
Volume = 22/7 x 7 x 7 x 10 = 1540 cubic cm.


Chapter 8: Statistics

The mean of a set of observations is the sum of all the observations divided by the number of observations. The
median of a set of observations is the middle value when the observations are arranged in ascending order; for an
even number of observations it is the average of the two middle values.

Example: Find the mean of the observations 4, 8, 6, 5, 7.
Mean = (4 + 8 + 6 + 5 + 7) / 5 = 30 / 5 = 6.

Example: Find the median of the observations 11, 7, 15, 3, 9.
In ascending order: 3, 7, 9, 11, 15. The median is the middle value, 9.


Chapter 9: Probability

Probability is a measure of how likely an event is to occur, defined as the number of favourable outcomes divided
by the total number of equally likely outcomes.

Example: A bag contains 5 red balls and 3 blue balls. One ball is drawn at random. Find the probability that it is
red.
Total outcomes = 5 + 3 = 8.
P(red) = 5 / 8.


Chapter 10: Sets

A set is a well-defined collection of distinct objects. The union of two sets A and B, written A union B, is the
set of all elements that are in A, in B, or in both. The intersection of two sets A and B, written A intersection B,
is the set of elements that are in both A and B.

Figure 6: A Venn diagram showing two overlapping circles labelled A and B, with the number of elements marked in
each region: only in A, only in B, in both A and B, and outside both sets.

Example: If a set A has 5 elements not in B, set B has 4 elements not in A, and 3 elements are common to both sets,
find the number of elements in A union B.
n(A union B) = 5 + 3 + 4 = 12.


Chapter 11: Commercial Mathematics

Profit and loss are calculated on the cost price of an article. If an article is bought for a cost price and sold
for a selling price higher than the cost price, the difference is called profit; if the selling price is lower, the
difference is called loss. Profit or loss is usually expressed as a percentage of the cost price.

Simple interest on a sum of money is calculated using the formula simple interest = (principal x rate x time) / 100,
where the rate is the annual rate of interest and the time is measured in years.

Example: Find the simple interest on Rs 5000 for 3 years at 8 per cent per annum.
Simple interest = (5000 x 8 x 3) / 100 = 1200.


Chapter 12: Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

A pair of simultaneous linear equations in two variables can be solved by substitution, elimination, or using
determinants (Cramer's rule). Two linear equations in x and y have a unique solution when their coefficients are
not proportional.

Example: Solve the pair of linear equations 2x + 3y = 13 and x - y = 1.
From the second equation, x = y + 1.

Chapter 13: Matrices and Determinants

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers arranged in rows and columns. The determinant of a 2 by 2 matrix with
entries a, b in the first row and c, d in the second row is calculated as ad - bc.

Example: Find the determinant of the matrix with rows (4, 3) and (2, 5).
Determinant = 4 x 5 - 3 x 2 = 20 - 6 = 14.


Chapter 14: Calculus - Derivatives and Integrals

The derivative of a function describes its rate of change. Using the power rule, the derivative of x raised to the
power n is n times x raised to the power (n - 1). The integral of a function is the reverse process of
differentiation, and the integral of x raised to the power n is x raised to the power (n + 1), divided by (n + 1).

Example: Find the derivative of y = x^3 + 2x.
dy/dx = 3x^2 + 2.

Example: Evaluate the integral of 3x^2 with respect to x.
Integral = x^3 + C.
